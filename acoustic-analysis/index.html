Ethogenesis Lab · Graduate course · practical

Acoustic analysis of marmoset vocalizations

From the microphone to a validated table of call measurements. The course is built on one real experiment, so every day builds on the one before.

Format13 days × 3 h (≈ 39 h)
Each day~1 h concepts · ~1.5 h lab · ~0.5 h discussion
ToolsPython (Jupyter), Audacity or Raven Lite
BringLaptop + good closed-back headphones
Before Day 1: enrolled students receive a step-by-step setup guide (30–45 min) to install Python and the course environment.

Goal. By the end, every student can take a raw recording to a validated table of call measurements without help, knows why each step is done, and can recognise when something has gone wrong.

Audience. Graduate students who record and analyse marmoset vocalizations. Python packages: numpy, scipy, pandas, matplotlib, scikit-learn, soundfile/librosa. Headphones matter: most marmoset call energy is at 5–10 kHz.

Part I — Foundations: from sound to spectrogram

Days 1–3

Day 1 From sound to spectrogram

Students already use spectrograms; today they learn what is underneath one: how sound becomes numbers, and how the Fourier transform turns numbers into frequencies. Short "check your understanding" slides (2–3 minutes, in pairs) close each topic; their answers are in the speaker notes. The finite piece and the spectrogram itself follow on Day 3.

Slides 1 · 55 min

  • The measurement chain: pressure → microphone → preamp → anti-alias filter → ADC.
  • Sampling and the Nyquist limit; aliasing (f_alias = |f − k·fs|), shown on a real twitter; the file header is not the bandwidth (webcam vs Zoom).
  • Quantisation and bit depth (≈ 6 dB per bit); dBFS and clipping.
  • The Fourier transform, built step by step: a sinusoid's three numbers (amplitude, frequency, phase); building any shape from sinusoids (square wave); the probe test and orthogonality; why two probes (cosine and sine) are needed, and why spectra are complex numbers (length = amplitude, angle = phase); analysis and synthesis (nothing is lost); from the continuous transform to the DFT (sampling makes the spectrum repeat; a finite piece gives bins); magnitude, power, dB.
  • The idea of the FFT: split into even and odd samples, solve each half, combine; N² becomes N·log₂N.
Notebook Part A · 35 min Inspect a real recording in dBFS. Make a 40 kHz tone alias to 8 kHz, alias a twitter and then prevent it. Requantise a phee to 8 and 4 bits and measure the noise. Build the Fourier transform by hand: assemble a square wave, sweep a probe to draw a spectrum, recover amplitude and phase with two probes, rebuild a phee exactly from its spectrum and hear what scrambling the phase does. Then write the DFT as a loop and a 10-line recursive FFT, and check both match np.fft.
Homework Characterise one of your own recordings: fs, bit depth, levels, true bandwidth, and write down the spectrogram settings of your usual software (judged on Day 3). Optional: the self-study notebook (listening tour of the repertoire and the course data).

Day 2 Acquisition — microphones, recorders, and the room

Concepts

  • Microphone types (condenser vs dynamic, measurement vs recording mics), polar patterns, sensitivity, self-noise. Frequency response: what the Zoom H4n Pro, smartphones and our webcams do to a call (measured webcam vs Zoom), and how to measure a response by substitution.
  • Recorders and preamps; gain staging; why distance dominates level (inverse-square law, ≈ −6 dB per doubling of distance). Air absorption: it grows with frequency and depends on temperature and humidity (ISO 9613-1), so a call's spectrum changes with distance; the speed of sound changes with temperature. What to log in the field.
  • Wavelength and head size: the marmoset head (≈ 25 mm wide in infants, 30–35 mm in adults) casts a sound shadow above ≈ 3–4 kHz; why small heads localise with level differences and hear high frequencies.
  • Room acoustics: reflections, reverberation in a small box, echoes that turn frequency sweeps into amplitude modulation, background noise (HVAC, cage, people, low-frequency rumble). Loudspeakers for playback: response, beaming (a 40 mm driver beams above ≈ 3 kHz), level calibration with a sound level meter, the playback chain (software, Bluetooth delay, speaker, room), preparing stimulus files, and pseudoreplication.
  • The lab's setup: two platforms joined by a 42 cm bridge, two Zoom H4n Pro recorders, three webcams, a JBL Go 2 speaker, and the closed-loop trigger (a separate trigger mic; calls of 150–500 ms are answered with a parent's trill, calls of 1–10 s with a phee).
  • Timing: speed of sound (34 cm per ms), cross-correlation, and checking every delay against the geometry.
Lab A (multimic, block I) Load the four Zoom channels of one block. Measure levels per channel, broadband and in the call band, and meet the 11 Hz rumble. Infant calls are ≈ 21 dB weaker on the adult platform's mic: what distance ratio would that be? Measure noise floors and the SNR of every call. Simulate a phee recorded with too little and too much gain.
Lab B (multimic, block III) Find every playback trill from the level difference between platforms, measure the trigger latency (≈ 0.4 s), and flag the detections that are really the speaker. Write a cross-correlation, test it on a planted delay, then measure the delay between platforms and check it against the geometry. Compare echo tails near and far, and the bandwidth of the three webcams. Audit the playbacks: level consistency, level at the infant, repeated stimuli, clicks. Test whether amplitude modulation comes from the animal or from echoes by comparing the same calls on four mics.
Exercise Draft a recording-session checklist for your own setup.

Day 3 A finite piece of sound — from the Fourier transform to the spectrogram

One idea for the whole day: we only ever analyse a finite piece of sound, often 4 ms. Every property of real spectra follows from it, and each one is derived from the probe test of Day 1, with real calls as examples.

Slides 1 · 55 min

  • The puzzle: one phee, one instant, three different spectra (1, 4 and 50 ms pieces: peaks 2000, 500 and 42 Hz wide).
  • Resolution: telling two frequencies apart takes time (beats), Δf = 1/T. Real case: two infants phee-ing 484 Hz apart blur into one band at 4 ms and separate at 20 ms.
  • Leakage from the probe test: on a piece with a non-whole number of cycles the products no longer cancel, so probes find energy the sound does not have. The same thing as seams (the DFT repeats the piece).
  • Convolution as a sliding weighted average (smoothing), and the Fourier pair: convolving in time = multiplying in frequency, and vice versa (shown on a smoothed phee envelope). So cutting = multiplying by a window = convolving the spectrum with the window's spectrum: every line of the call gets a copy of it (main lobe = blur, side lobes = leakage).
  • Real case: a phee's harmonic, 45 dB below the fundamental, is buried by leakage without a window and stands out with Hann.
  • Windows: the remedy and its price (wider peak, ≈ 3/T to separate two tones); rectangular, Hann, Hamming, Blackman compared; use Hann by default.
Notebook Part A · 40 min Beats; the probe test on a 2 ms piece (whole vs 4.5 cycles); seams; convolution by hand and checked against the FFT product; window spectra; the hidden harmonic of a real phee found again with a window; two tones; mix two real phees and find the shortest window that separates them.

Slides 2 · 45 min

  • Bins: the picket fence and scalloping (a call between bins reads up to 1.4 dB low with Hann); zero-padding adds points, not resolution.
  • The uncertainty principle, σt·σf ≥ 1/(4π), for windows and for sounds themselves: a 0.5 ms sound is 4 kHz wide, so a click is broadband because it is short.
  • The spectrogram built frame by frame on a real trill (cut, window, Fourier transform, stack); the five settings and what each really changes; choosing the window from the biology (trill FM cycle ≈ 43 ms → 4 ms window); colour scales.
  • Read along a row, a spectrogram is a bank of band-pass filters shaped like the window's spectrum: the bridge to Day 4.
Notebook Part B · 40 min Picket fence and scalloping for three windows; zero-padding; measuring σt·σf; short sounds are broad. Then build my_spectrogram() from scratch, one step per cell, and verify it against SciPy. Apply it to a trill with 1, 4 and 20 ms windows and measure its modulation period; overlap and colour range; show that one spectrogram row equals the output of a band-pass filter.
Homework (1) Judge the spectrogram settings of your usual software (Δf, time step). (2) Quality-control report (graded): a function that checks a whole folder of WAVs for sample rate, bit depth, duration, clipping, DC offset, dropouts, level, true bandwidth and low-frequency rumble, run on the course data and on your own recordings.

Part II — Deeper signal analysis

Days 4–7

Day 4 Filters and noise

Concepts

  • From Day 3: each spectrogram row is already a band-pass filter. A filter changes each frequency's amplitude and phase.
  • Characterising noise: PSD of silent stretches; stationary vs transient noise; the 11 Hz rumble.
  • Filters: high-, low- and band-pass; FIR vs IIR; order and steepness; phase distortion and zero-phase filtering (filtfilt); ringing (a sharp filter rings long: the uncertainty principle again); edge effects. What filtering can and cannot fix (noise inside the call band, echoes).
  • Signal-to-noise ratio: definitions and estimation. How the upstream classifier picked the better microphone for each detection by SNR.
  • Playback blocks: the speaker's sound also reaches the infant's microphone. Spotting and handling playback leakage.
Lab (full session) Compute the noise PSD for each block. Design the high-pass that removes the rumble and a band-pass for marmoset calls, and check what each removes. Estimate the SNR of each call and compare it with snr_conjunto_*. Find the playback events in blocks III and VI.

Day 5 The signal in time — envelopes and modulation

Concepts

  • Instantaneous amplitude: rectify-and-smooth, RMS, and the analytic signal (Hilbert transform); instantaneous frequency.
  • Amplitude and frequency modulation; the modulation spectrum.
  • Temporal measures: duration, onset/offset, rise and decay time, inter-call interval (ICI), and how thresholds change them.
Lab Compute envelopes of a 2-min Block I recording with three methods and compare them. Measure durations and ICIs; see how the threshold changes the numbers. Measure the AM and FM rates of trills and twitters with the Hilbert transform, and compare with the spectrogram-based estimate from Day 3. Tell modulation made by the animal from modulation made by the room (Day 2).

Day 6 Spectral estimation in depth

Concepts

  • Window families beyond Day 3 (Kaiser, DPSS tapers): choosing the trade-off between main-lobe width and side-lobe level on purpose.
  • A spectrum from noisy data is itself noisy: averaging (Welch), the bias–variance trade-off, multitaper estimates.
  • Harmonic structure: the cepstrum, and why it underlies harmonic-to-noise ratio (HNR) and cepstral peak prominence (CPP).
Lab Compare windows on a weak harmonic next to a strong fundamental. Estimate the background-noise spectrum of a silent stretch with a single FFT, with Welch, and with multitaper. Compute the cepstrum of a phee and find its fundamental.

Day 7 Advanced time–frequency analysis

Concepts

  • Beyond the standard STFT: multitaper spectrograms, reassigned spectrograms, and a brief look at wavelets and constant-Q transforms.
  • Choosing analysis parameters per call type, and reporting them so others can reproduce your figures.
  • Display for publication: fixed dB scales, colour maps, axis choices.
Lab Extend my_spectrogram() with window choice and a fixed dB scale. Compare standard, multitaper and reassigned spectrograms of a trill and a twitter. Produce a publication-ready figure of one call of each type, with a methods sentence describing the settings.

Part III — Preparing the data

Days 8–9

Day 8 Pre-processing — detection and segmentation

Concepts

  • Energy-based detection within a frequency band; thresholds relative to the noise floor; hysteresis, minimum duration, gap merging.
  • Units of analysis: syllable (sub-pulse) → call → sequence, and the thresholds that define them (e.g. ICI < 3.8 s for sequences).
  • Evaluation: matching detections to reference annotations; precision, recall, onset/offset tolerance.
Lab Build a band-limited energy detector, like the closed-loop playback trigger. Run it on the full session, evaluate it against the classifier annotations, and tune it.
Mini-project assigned. Choose a question and a data set (ideally your own recordings) to analyse with the course pipeline. Presentations on Day 13.

Day 9 Automatic labelling and its pitfalls

Concepts

  • What deep classifiers (TweetyNet/Twitinety, AVES) do, conceptually; label codes; class probabilities and confidence.
  • Overlapping calls, classifier "mistake" classes, and labels that turn out to be noise. Why human validation is still required.
Lab Blind-listen to the pitfall_* clips and label them yourselves. Compare your labels with the classifier's and inspect the class-probability columns. Spot-check one call type and estimate the classifier's error rate.

Part IV — Measuring and interpreting calls

Days 10–13

Day 10 Feature extraction I — frequency contours

Concepts

  • Pitch and dominant-frequency tracking: peak picking per frame, tonality gating, continuity constraints, smoothing splines.
  • Classic failures: octave and harmonic jumps, tracking noise in gaps, spline overshoot and undershoot at the edges. How the lab fixed them: per-call-type frequency bands, no extrapolation, clamping.
Lab Implement a simplified contour tracker in Python. Compare it with the saved lab contours (contours_course_wavs.csv.gz). Reproduce a harmonic jump by widening the band.

Day 11 Feature extraction II — the feature set and quality control

Concepts

  • The 36 features: contour-derived (min/max/mean frequency, slope, modulation), power and tonality, spectral shape (centroid, flatness, entropy…), temporal envelope (attack, decay, AM), voice quality (HNR, CPP).
  • Quality control: per-call-type outlier detection, pass/fail criteria, sanity checks on value ranges. Case study: a real bug that produced contour values of ±10⁸ Hz.
Lab Compute about 8 features yourself for the development calls and check them against features_course_wavs.csv. Screen the feature table for impossible values. Apply the per-call-type criteria.

Day 12 From measurements to biology

Concepts

  • Exploring feature space: standardisation, PCA, LDA, UMAP/t-SNE; separating call types.
  • The animal is the unit: individual voice, pseudoreplication, leave-one-animal-out validation. Case study: a naive classifier scored A-vs-B AUC ≈ 0.80; holding out whole animals dropped it to chance.
  • Rates vs counts (block durations differ); developmental trajectories; a first look at mixed models.
  • Hand-crafted features vs deep embeddings: what each one is good for.
Lab Run PCA and LDA on phee/trill/twitter (features_noview_phee_trill_twitter.csv). Compare naive and leave-one-animal-out cross-validation. Plot phee and trill rates across PND for Pink3A and Blue3A from session_index.csv.

Day 13 Reproducible analysis and project presentations

  • Organising a project: raw data read-only, scripts instead of clicks, parameters in one place, versioned outputs, a README a newcomer can follow.
  • Mini-project presentations (10 min each + discussion).
