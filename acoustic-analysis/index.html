Ethogenesis Lab · Graduate course · practical

Acoustic analysis of marmoset vocalizations

From the microphone to a validated table of call measurements. The course is built on one real experiment, so every day builds on the one before.

Format13 days × 3 h (≈ 39 h)
Each day~1 h concepts · ~1.5 h lab · ~0.5 h discussion
ToolsPython (Jupyter), Audacity or Raven Lite
BringLaptop + good closed-back headphones

Goal. By the end, every student can take a raw recording to a validated table of call measurements without help, knows why each step is done, and can recognise when something has gone wrong.

Audience. Graduate students who record and analyse marmoset vocalizations. Python packages: numpy, scipy, pandas, matplotlib, scikit-learn, soundfile/librosa. Headphones matter: most marmoset call energy is at 5–10 kHz.

The course data

All labs use a developmental playback study (Firmino thesis, 2026). Infant marmosets were recorded from postnatal day (PND) 7 to 127 and received contingent vocal feedback from a loudspeaker. The core pair is Pink3A (Group A, control) and Blue3A (Group B, switch), recorded on the same days.

Part I — Getting the signal right

Days 1–3

Day 1 Sound, hearing, and the marmoset repertoire

Concepts

  • What sound is: pressure waves, frequency, amplitude, the decibel. Why marmoset calls (phee, trill, twitter, tsik, cry…) sit mostly at 5–15 kHz.
  • The experiment behind the course data: blocks I–VII, Groups A/B, Playback vs Observation, file-naming convention.
Lab Set up the Python environment. Load a WAV and check its sample rate, bit depth and duration. Plot the waveform. Listen to each call type in clips/ and describe it.
Exercise Write a one-paragraph acoustic description of each call type, as for a methods section.

Day 2 Acquisition I — microphones, recorders, and the room

Concepts

  • Microphone types (condenser vs dynamic, measurement vs recording mics), polar patterns, frequency response, sensitivity, self-noise.
  • Recorders and preamps; gain staging; why distance dominates level (inverse-square law, ≈ −6 dB per doubling of distance).
  • Room acoustics: reflections, reverberation in a small box, background noise (HVAC, cage, people). Loudspeakers for playback: their frequency response limits what the animal actually hears.
  • The lab's setup: two platforms, two Zoom H4n recorders, monitoring webcam, JBL speaker, and the closed-loop trigger (energy at 5–10 kHz plus a duration window).
Lab (multimic) Load all synchronised channels of one block and compare RMS and peak levels. Audio-01/02 are on the infant's platform and Audio-03/04 on the adult's platform, ≈ 22 dB lower for infant calls. How much of that is distance? Find one call on every channel and compare how it looks and sounds.
Exercise Draft a recording-session checklist for your own setup.

Day 3 Acquisition II — from pressure to numbers

Concepts

  • Sampling and the Nyquist limit; anti-alias filtering; quantisation, bit depth and dynamic range (≈ 6 dB per bit); dBFS; clipping; DC offset.
  • File formats and compression. The file header is not the bandwidth.
  • Calibration: what you can and cannot say without a calibrated level (dBFS ≠ dB SPL).
Lab
  1. The webcam channels report 48 kHz. Measure their spectrum above 15 kHz. What was the real recording bandwidth, and which calls are damaged?
  2. Downsample a twitter to 16 kHz without an anti-alias filter; listen and look for aliasing. Repeat with proper resampling.
  3. Requantise a phee to 8 and 4 bits and measure the noise floor.
  4. Run an automatic quality-control check on all course WAVs: sample rate, % clipped samples, DC offset, peak and RMS level.
Deliverable A QC report function you'll reuse on your own recordings.

Part II — Looking at signals

Days 4–6

Day 4 The signal in time

Concepts

  • Waveform, instantaneous amplitude, envelope (rectify-and-smooth, RMS, Hilbert), dB conversion.
  • Temporal measures: duration, onset/offset, rise and decay time, inter-call interval (ICI).
Lab Compute the envelope of a 2-min Block I recording. Mark the calls by eye, then measure duration and ICI. Compare one infant at PND 8 and PND 84.

Day 5 The frequency domain

Concepts

  • Intuition for Fourier analysis; FFT; magnitude spectrum; power spectral density (Welch).
  • Harmonics and the fundamental; frequency vs amplitude modulation; windows and spectral leakage; zero padding vs real resolution.
Lab Synthesise signals that mimic marmoset calls: a pure tone, an upward FM sweep (≈ phee), sinusoidal FM (≈ trill), a harmonic stack. Analyse them, then the real clips. Find the fundamental and harmonics of a phee.

Day 6 The spectrogram

Concepts

  • Short-time Fourier transform; the time–frequency trade-off (Δt·Δf); window length, overlap, NFFT. Display choices (dB range, colour map) can hide structure, or invent it.
  • Why the lab uses a 4 ms Hann window with 75 % overlap.
Lab Make spectrograms of the same trill and phee with 1, 4 and 20 ms windows. Reproduce the lab-standard spectrogram. Build a reusable plot_spectrogram() function with a kHz axis and call-onset markers.
Exercise Pick the best window for each call type and justify your choice.

Part III — Preparing the data

Days 7–9

Day 7 Pre-processing I — noise and filtering

Concepts

  • Characterising noise: PSD of silent stretches; stationary vs transient noise.
  • Filters: high-, low- and band-pass; FIR vs IIR; phase distortion and zero-phase filtering (filtfilt); edge effects. What filtering can and cannot fix.
  • Signal-to-noise ratio: definitions and estimation. How the upstream classifier picked the better microphone for each detection by SNR.
  • Playback blocks: the speaker's sound also reaches the infant's microphone. Spotting and handling playback leakage.
Lab (full session) Compute the noise PSD for each block. Design a band-pass filter for marmoset calls and check what it removes. Estimate the SNR of each call and compare it with snr_conjunto_*. Find the playback events in blocks III and VI.

Day 8 Pre-processing II — detection and segmentation

Concepts

  • Energy-based detection within a frequency band; thresholds relative to the noise floor; hysteresis, minimum duration, gap merging.
  • Units of analysis: syllable (sub-pulse) → call → sequence, and the thresholds that define them (e.g. ICI < 3.8 s for sequences).
  • Evaluation: matching detections to reference annotations; precision, recall, onset/offset tolerance.
Lab Build a band-limited energy detector, like the closed-loop playback trigger. Run it on the full session, evaluate it against the classifier annotations, and tune it.
Mini-project assigned. Choose a question and a data set (ideally your own recordings) to analyse with the course pipeline. Presentations on Day 13.

Day 9 Automatic labelling and its pitfalls

Concepts

  • What deep classifiers (TweetyNet/Twitinety, AVES) do, conceptually; label codes; class probabilities and confidence.
  • Overlapping calls, classifier "mistake" classes, and labels that turn out to be noise. Why human validation is still required.
Lab Blind-listen to the pitfall_* clips and label them yourselves. Compare your labels with the classifier's and inspect the class-probability columns. Spot-check one call type and estimate the classifier's error rate.

Part IV — Measuring and interpreting calls

Days 10–13

Day 10 Feature extraction I — frequency contours

Concepts

  • Pitch and dominant-frequency tracking: peak picking per frame, tonality gating, continuity constraints, smoothing splines.
  • Classic failures: octave and harmonic jumps, tracking noise in gaps, spline overshoot and undershoot at the edges. How the lab fixed them: per-call-type frequency bands, no extrapolation, clamping.
Lab Implement a simplified contour tracker in Python. Compare it with the saved lab contours (contours_course_wavs.csv.gz). Reproduce a harmonic jump by widening the band.

Day 11 Feature extraction II — the feature set and quality control

Concepts

  • The 36 features: contour-derived (min/max/mean frequency, slope, modulation), power and tonality, spectral shape (centroid, flatness, entropy…), temporal envelope (attack, decay, AM), voice quality (HNR, CPP).
  • Quality control: per-call-type outlier detection, pass/fail criteria, sanity checks on value ranges. Case study: a real bug that produced contour values of ±10⁸ Hz.
Lab Compute about 8 features yourself for the development calls and check them against features_course_wavs.csv. Screen the feature table for impossible values. Apply the per-call-type criteria.

Day 12 From measurements to biology

Concepts

  • Exploring feature space: standardisation, PCA, LDA, UMAP/t-SNE; separating call types.
  • The animal is the unit: individual voice, pseudoreplication, leave-one-animal-out validation. Case study: a naive classifier scored A-vs-B AUC ≈ 0.80; holding out whole animals dropped it to chance.
  • Rates vs counts (block durations differ); developmental trajectories; a first look at mixed models.
  • Hand-crafted features vs deep embeddings: what each one is good for.
Lab Run PCA and LDA on phee/trill/twitter (features_noview_phee_trill_twitter.csv). Compare naive and leave-one-animal-out cross-validation. Plot phee and trill rates across PND for Pink3A and Blue3A from session_index.csv.

Day 13 Reproducible analysis and project presentations

  • Organising a project: raw data read-only, scripts instead of clicks, parameters in one place, versioned outputs, a README a newcomer can follow.
  • Mini-project presentations (10 min each + discussion).

Suggested reading

  • Bradbury & Vehrencamp, Principles of Animal Communication: chapters on sound production and propagation.
  • Hopp, Owren & Evans (eds.), Animal Acoustic Communication: Sound Analysis and Research Methods (Springer, 1998).
  • Raven Pro user manual: chapters on spectrograms and measurements (free; a clear practical introduction to the STFT).
  • Stowell (2022), "Computational bioacoustics with deep learning: a review and roadmap", PeerJ.
  • Marmoset vocal repertoire and development: Agamaite et al. (2015, JASA); Pistorio, Vintch & Wang (2006, JASA); Takahashi et al. (2015, Science).
